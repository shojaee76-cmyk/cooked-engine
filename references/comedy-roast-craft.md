# Comedy Craft for Roasts: A Writing Doctrine for COOKED

> **Written for the COOKED project** — a receipt printer that roasts a person's crypto wallet and X account using only measured, public numbers. This is the roast engine's doctrine: every section is a rule an agent can apply to a line before it ships.
>
> **Primary sources drawn on:** Mel Helitzer with Mark Shatz, *Comedy Writing Secrets* 2nd ed. (full text mirrored [here](https://simonvirtualbooks.wordpress.com/2017/08/05/comedy-writing-secrets-2nd-edition-by-mel-helitzer-with-mark-shatz/)); Joe Toplyn's [How to Write a Roast](https://joetoplyn.com/how-to-write-a-roast), [How to Write a Joke](https://joetoplyn.com/how-to-write-a-joke), and the Witscript paper ([arXiv:2302.02008](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02008)); Greg Dean's joke structure via his [glossary](https://stand-upcomedy.com/glossary/joke) and Scotty Meltzer's [structural analysis](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-nice-structure); Emily McTernan, [The Ethics of Offensive Comedy](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/royal-institute-of-philosophy-supplements/article/ethics-of-offensive-comedy-punching-down-and-the-duties-of-comedians/A5B6FAAD512460544CB5A4D3127DE96A) (Cambridge, 2025).

## 1. Joke anatomy: what must be true before a line is a joke

A joke is a two-part machine. Greg Dean names five mechanisms between the halves: **Setup** (leads the audience one way), **Target Assumption** (the belief the setup installs), **Connector** (the one thing with two interpretations), **Reinterpretation** (the unexpected second meaning), **Punchline** (expresses the reinterpretation) ([glossary](https://stand-upcomedy.com/glossary/joke)). The setup builds a *first story*; the punchline forces a *second*. Meltzer renders the same spine Setup–Assumption–Connector–Shatter–Punch and notes connectors "don't have to be words" — they can be numbers, objects or attitudes ([IJA](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-nice-structure)).

Dean's *Joke Mine* drills from a premise through assumptions and reinterpretations to a punchline; equivalently you can write forward from setup, out from the middle, or backwards from the punchline — but Meltzer warns all three "make it easy to write puns and lots of really crappy jokes."

**For COOKED:** the setup is a measured number; the connector is a word in the metric's name carrying a second meaning (`gas`, `rug`, `hold`, `dump`, `locked`, `mint`, `burn`); the punchline reinterprets that word rather than restating the figure. Helitzer: humor "is written backwards," so build from the punchline, and the third element must be the surprise ([quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/50468-comedy-writing-secrets)).

**One-liner vs two-sentence.** A one-liner welds setup and punchline — Helitzer: "A joke is not a short story. It's a small story—often a single-sentence story." Receipts favour it: the eye moves, so the line must land before it scrolls. Use two sentences only when the number itself is the funniest thing.

## 2. Helitzer's pillars, applied

Two engines drive laughter — **surprise** and **superiority**; "If there's no surprise, there's no laugh." Superiority is why roast works: "the greater the prestige of the victim, the greater our desire to equalize," and "the target of a roast smiles only because he knows everyone is watching for his approval" ([CWS](https://simonvirtualbooks.wordpress.com/2017/08/05/comedy-writing-secrets-2nd-edition-by-mel-helitzer-with-mark-shatz/)).

The recipe is **THREES** — Target, Hostility, Realism, Exaggeration, Emotion, Surprise ([Barking Up The Wrong Tree](https://bakadesuyo.com/2013/11/how-to-be-funny), quoting the book). Three matter for a receipt:

- **Target:** "Humor is criticism cloaked as entertainment and directed at a specific target… If there's no corpse, there's usually no joke."
- **Realism:** "Most good jokes state a bitter truth" (Larry Gelbart). Exaggeration must be *logical*: "Simply being ludicrous or audacious is not a skill. It's amateur."
- **KISS:** "use the KISS (Keep It Simple, Stupid) approach and only focus on a few key points." Helitzer's speech rule is the print rule: "If a sentence is longer than fourteen or so words, edit it down."

**Rule of three.** Two straight lines build tension; the third explodes, "logically related to the first two lines, but unexpected and exaggerated" ([Writer's Digest](https://www.writersdigest.com/there-are-no-rules/comedy-writing-secrets-triples)). "If you want to be seen—stand up! If you want to be heard—speak up! If you want to be appreciated—shut up!" — drop the first line and the punch softens. Hard cap: three jokes per subject per monologue.

**Specificity.** General targets fail; narrow the premise. A vague "a man" earns no laugh, but naming a target adds realism and weight — and specificity is what makes a claim checkable.

**Insult comedy's one unforgivable move.** Helitzer's insult axiom is "Insult only ugly people and ignoramuses," and its logic matters: attack behaviour or choice, never an immutable trait. Put-down humour "is acceptable when the joke is in the cleverness of the response." And the absolute rule: **"Never apologize… Also, don't explain… If you have to explain a joke, don't use it."** Hedging and explanation are the only ways a line dies twice.

## 3. Toplyn's roast method

Toplyn treats a roast as joke-writing with one fixed topic: *"I'm paying tribute to [Name]"* ([how-to-write-a-roast](https://joetoplyn.com/how-to-write-a-roast)).

1. **Association list.** Everything an audience would accept as true of the target — "the main building blocks of jokes, so the more associations a roastee has, the easier it will be." Roasting forbids the flattering ones: "come up with plenty of associations that are unflattering, embarrassing, or negative."
2. **Punch Line Makers.** Six algorithms for turning an association into a punchline. Verified: **#1 link two associations of the topic**; **#2 link the topic to pop culture**; **#3 ask a question about the topic**; **#4 find a play on words in the topic**. Write so the "laugh triggers are negative associations of the roastee."
3. **Anti-repetition.** Let no two jokes lean on the same association: include "jokes that rely on as many different associations as possible" and cut the ones that repeat — the most important rule for a printer emitting a *page* of lines.
4. **Joke Maximizers.** Post-draft polish. Verified: **#4 "Make everything clear"** (guess the wrong handle and nobody laughs), **#8 "Wildly exaggerate"**, **#11 "Don't be too on-the-nose."** Toplyn's teaching case: the draft called Ann Coulter a scarecrow; the shipped line was "Ann Coulter, if you're here, who's scaring the crows away from our crops!?" Same insult, indirect, therefore surprising.

**Numbers as setup, done properly.** Toplyn inserted "67" in his snail joke because that detail "subtly reinforces that this incident actually occurred," while deleting "African" as "distractingly specific and unnecessary" ([how-to-write-a-joke](https://joetoplyn.com/how-to-write-a-joke)). Rule: **a figure earns its place by making the claim feel audited, not by being large.**

**Punch up, or don't punch.** Non-celebrity: "keep your roast jokes fairly gentle. Joke about associations of the roastee that neither the roastee nor your audience is likely to be sensitive about. And make sure the roastee has agreed to be roasted… That way the audience won't feel too guilty to laugh." Celebrity: "your jokes can be a lot harsher because in that case you're 'punching up'… It's doubly okay because the celebrity has obviously agreed to be roasted." Consent is simultaneously the ethics and the laugh-enabler.

McTernan's philosophical backing: "don't punch down" misdirects attention; ask **what a joke does** in context — whether it undermines anti-discriminatory norms or "reinforces unjust hierarchies and damaging stereotypes" — and note that **the audience is a crucial, often determining factor** ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/royal-institute-of-philosophy-supplements/article/ethics-of-offensive-comedy-punching-down-and-the-duties-of-comedians/A5B6FAAD512460544CB5A4D3127DE96A)). Punching up targets holders of more power, down targets those with less, sideways is self-deprecation ([Dallas Comedy Club](https://dallas-comedyclub.com/punching-up-sideways-and-never-down)).

**For COOKED:** a wallet is not a celebrity and not a person. The only safe surface is **behaviour visible in public data** — gas paid, tokens farmed and abandoned, buys against sells, dust airdrops, follow-back ratios. Never identity, nationality, or appearance.

## 4. Roast-specific tactics worth stealing

- **Insincere compliment.** Praise straight-faced, then reverse. Helitzer's canonical reversal: "Goldie Hawn is funny, sexy, beautiful, talented, intelligent, warm, and consistently sunny. Other than that, she doesn't impress me at all" (Neil Simon).
- **Misdirection.** Sound like a compliment, a fact, or a receipt line, then land elsewhere — roast writers call it the most recurring technique in roast jokes ([How to Write Roast Jokes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i_DMSWD3GWs)).
- **Callback and escalation.** Reuse an earlier image with a new meaning, or raise the same premise's stakes across three beats.
- **Understatement.** Say the devastating thing flatly; let the reader do the arithmetic — exaggeration's band stretched "a little."
- **Bureaucratic misregistration.** File the human disaster under a procedure — a compliance event, a form letter, an audit finding. The register of *Airplane!*, *Monty Python*, every deadpan institution ([Deadpan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deadpan)).
- **The "you look like a" box.** Compare the target to an object drawn from its *own* associations — a farm wallet looks like a tip jar with a mortgage — never a generic insult file.
- **Specificity beats adjectives.** "Rekt" is an adjective; "0.0041 ETH of gas to lose $180" is a joke.
- **Indifference beats anger (the COOKED sci-fi rule).** Kubrick wanted HAL to sound "neither patronizing, nor… intimidating, nor… pompous"; a voice researcher described HAL as "a mix between a butler and a psychoanalyst… a sense of deference and of detachment" ([WRAL/NYT](https://www.wral.com/archive/17452449)). GLaDOS works identically: synthesised, unbothered delivery for monstrous lines ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GLaDOS)). Deadpan theory gives the mechanism: material with a comic mismatch, a performer who refuses to underline it, and an audience with enough context ([VFS](https://vfs.edu/news/deadpan)). **A printer that sounds angry reads as a bad chatbot. A printer that reads the numbers out flatly, as if reading them is its job, reads as a verdict.**

## 5. Failure modes

| Failure | Looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Punching down | A line about who the subject is, not what the wallet did | Re-base on a measured number |
| Identity over behaviour | Names, nationality, appearance, assumed politics | Only public ledger and public X activity |
| Joke explanation | "because 42% of your trades…" | If it needs the clause, cut the clause and the joke |
| Punchline bloat | Setup lands the laugh before the last word | Move the funniest word to the end; cut to one line |
| Repeated association | Three gas jokes on one receipt | One joke per association; three per subject |
| "You are an idiot" non-jokes | No connector, no target assumption | Score 0; regenerate with a different connector |
| Harassment cosplay | Sustained aggression with no craft | Add consent; cruelty must be proportionate |
| Hedging | "no offence", "just saying", apologies | Never apologize; never explain |
| Unearned cruelty | Insult harsher than the numbers support | Exaggerate only logically — reality must carry it |

## 6. The receipt format

**Numbers are the setup, not the punchline.** A figure like `-94.2%` is the first story; the second comes from a word doing double duty in the metric's name. The last word of the line must be the surprise.

**The reader is the subject.** Toplyn's roast topic is a tribute to a named target; Helitzer's MAPP (material, audience, performer, purpose) says material must fit performer and audience. Here the performer is a printer and the audience is one person holding paper: second person, address the holder, never a crowd.

**Deadpan forensic beats hype.** Hype register ("SAVAGE. REKT. NGMI.") asserts how funny the line is — the one claim a roast can never make about itself. Forensic register — measured, dated, indifferent, procedural — needs no claim, and it enforces truthfulness: the format implies an audit, so one invented number poisons the page.

**Shipped receipt shape:** 5–11 lines, each one sentence or two short ones; at most three lines share a subject; one line is an insincere compliment; the last line callbacks the first.

## 7. LINE EDITING CHECKLIST (run on every generated line)

1. **Is there a measured number in the setup?** If the line has no public data under it, it is not a COOKED line.
2. **Can I name the connector** — the single word or figure read two ways? If not, there is no joke.
3. **Is the target assumption different from the reinterpretation?** If the reader can only read it one way, regenerate.
4. **Is the last word the funniest / most surprising word?** Move it; never let the surprise sit mid-sentence.
5. **Does the line survive without its explanatory clause?** Delete the clause and re-read. If it dies, it was never alive.
6. **Is it one sentence, or two short ones?** Over ~14 words, cut (Helitzer).
7. **Is the attack on behaviour, not identity?** Any line about who someone is gets deleted, not softened.
8. **Would the target, reading the receipt, find it fair?** If the only defence is "it's just a joke," cut it.
9. **Has this association been used already on this receipt?** One joke per association; three per subject maximum.
10. **Is the exaggeration logical** — traceable to a real figure? Ludicrous without arithmetic is amateur.
11. **Is the register indifferent rather than angry?** Remove exclamation marks, data-free insults, and second-person aggression.
12. **If this is a triple, is the payoff third and the loudest?** Two straight setups, one turn.
13. **Does an implied comparison ("you look like a") draw from the target's own numbers** rather than a generic insult inventory?
14. **Would a stranger with no context still get it?** If it depends on earlier lines, move it later or make the callback deliberate.
15. **Is it true as printed?** Every number must be checkable against the source query. If unverifiable, it ships without a number or does not ship.

## 8. Scoring rubric (0–5 per axis, 30 max; ship at ≥ 21, no axis below 3)

| Axis | 0 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Specificity** | Generic insult, no number | A number, but a common one | A number nobody else would have printed |
| **Surprise** | Last word predictable | Mild shift | Genuine reinterpretation of the connector |
| **Brevity** | Two+ sentences of run-up | One sentence, slightly long | Every word load-bearing; punch at the end |
| **Cruelty-with-consent** | Identity, harassment-shaped | Behaviour, proportionate | Behaviour, and funny enough to survive the target quoting it |
| **Shareability** | Nobody screenshots it | Quotable | The holder photographs the receipt and posts it |
| **Truthfulness** | Unverifiable or invented | Accurate but loosely sourced | Exact, auditable, matches the printed figure |

A line scoring 5 on cruelty and 2 on truthfulness is not a joke; it is the failure that makes the whole page untrustworthy.
